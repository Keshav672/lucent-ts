LMTY vs. the Tea Giants

How the 20 biggest tea brands grew, why their buyers chose them, and what a one-product dropper brand should copy. Includes a full SWOT of LMTY's store, product and marketing as they stand today.

Built from company filings, UK Companies House accounts, Circana/IRI category data cited in trade press, and brand histories. Figures are the latest public numbers found; private brands only publish estimates, and those are marked.

Bottom line. LMTY is not competing with Lipton on tea. Lipton sells a 2-cent tea bag; LMTY sells a 10-second ritual that replaces a $5+ café drink (the Starbucks "medicine ball"). Every big tea brand that won did it by owning one occasion and one feeling with one hero product, then buying distribution. The ones that failed (Teavana, Honest Tea, Snapple under Quaker) lost the occasion or the channel.

LMTY's hardest problems are the ones money and time solve slowly: nobody knows the name yet, the dropper format is unfamiliar, it costs more per cup than a tea bag, and it isn't in stores. The plan below is built to work around those, not pretend they don't exist.

LMTY SWOT: store, product, marketing

Based on the live store (shoplumenity.com), the drops product created this week (1 / 3 / 6 bottle at $20.99 / $41.97 / $65.94), the earlier tea research report, and what is known about the supplier listing.

Strengths

  • A clear, borrowed occasion. "The café medicine ball, at home" is an occasion people already pay $5+ for. It went viral on TikTok in 2016 and Starbucks made it official in 2017.
  • Portable format. A 60 mL dropper works anywhere there is water. No tea brand in the top 20 owns this.
  • Bundle economics built in. Buy 2 Get 1 / Buy 3 Get 3 lifts order value to $41.97–$65.94, above the $40 free-shipping line.
  • Fast, focused storefront modelled on proven DTC pages (Pinemoor, Auna): one product, one story, tested cart.

Weaknesses

  • Nobody knows the name. Lipton has been on shelves for over a century. When a shopper is unsure, they pick the brand they recognize, and LMTY starts at zero awareness. That takes years and real ad money to change.
  • No track record of trust. Big brands carry decades of "it was fine last time". LMTY has no history, no word of mouth and no repeat buyers yet, and that only builds one customer at a time.
  • Drops are an unfamiliar way to drink tea. People know what a tea bag or a bottle is. A dropper reads as a supplement or a flavoring to many shoppers, so part of every ad has to teach the format before it can sell it.
  • Costs more than the habit it replaces. About $0.37–$0.70 per serving vs. about $0.05–$0.20 for a supermarket tea bag. It wins against the café cup but loses against the pantry box.
  • Not where people already shop. Lipton is in every grocery store and gas station today; LMTY is online only and takes 3–5 days to arrive. Getting onto shelves takes sales data, retailer relationships and slotting money.
  • Easy to copy. The bottle is a white-label supplier product. A rival, including Lipton/Tazo, could launch the same drops quickly and outspend you on ads.
  • One product, one flavor. A shopper who doesn't like lemon has nothing else to try, and the whole business rides on a single item.

Opportunities

  • Drink-mix format is booming. Powdered and liquid mixes grew double digits four years running, +20% in 2024 to a $1.5B US category (Circana via CNN). Liquid I.V. went from about $200M to nearly $1B under Unilever.
  • Functional and better-for-you RTD tea is the fastest-growing tea segment; zero-sugar is where Pure Leaf and others are launching.
  • Seasonality you can plan around: cold-and-flu season, back-to-office, travel.
  • Flavor line extensions (ginger, peach, chai) once lemon proves out, the way Celestial built off Sleepytime.

Threats

  • Lipton/Tazo already sell the medicine-ball concentrate at mass retail for about $6 (Target), and the owner (CVC's Lipton Teas & Infusions) can copy any format.
  • Regulation: health claims ("cancel out flu season") and fake reviews; the FTC review rule allows about $53,000 per violation.
  • Paid-social economics: the research report modeled a CPA of $25–$35 against a thin per-order margin.
  • Tariffs and cross-border shipping costs on an imported product (DAVIDsTEA lost 28% of US sales in a quarter to tariffs and de minimis changes).

Why someone would pick LMTY over Lipton, or not

They pick LMTY when…

  • They already buy the café honey-citrus drink and want it cheaper and faster.
  • They are away from a kitchen: office, gym, travel, car.
  • They want zero sugar but the café flavor (if verified).
  • They found it through a creator they trust, and it looks like a "thing", not a tea bag.
  • The bundle makes the per-cup price feel fair ($10.99/bottle on Buy 3 Get 3).

They stay with Lipton when…

  • They drink tea out of habit, at home, and price per cup matters most.
  • They trust a 130-year-old name more than a new one with no reviews.
  • They can buy it today in the supermarket; LMTY takes 3–5 days to ship.
  • They want "real tea", not drops (perceived as a supplement or flavoring).
  • They don't connect drops with the medicine-ball moment; the ad never reached them.

Implication: LMTY's real competitors are the Starbucks cup and other drink mixes (Liquid I.V., MiO), not Lipton's tea bag. Compare yourself to the café on every page, which the site already does ("Skip the drive-thru").

How they grew

Lipton: scale, then decline under debt

DistributionBrand iconCategory declineLipton became the world's #1 tea brand by putting one consistent product into every grocery and hotel worldwide, with Unilever's distribution. But black-tea bags have been shrinking in mature markets. Unilever sold the 34-brand tea business (about $2.26B revenue in 2020) to CVC for €4.5B in 2022. Revenue then fell to €1.57B in 2024 (−9.7%), and in 2026 CVC injected €210M to avoid a debt restructuring.

For LMTY: the giant is weak exactly where you play. Lipton sells the habit, not new occasions, and is cash-constrained. A niche format can grow under its nose.

Yorkshire Tea: one voice, sustained for a decade

Brand voicePremium priceIn 2015 Yorkshire Tea had about 14% share. It hired Lucky Generals and ran the same tongue-in-cheek "Where everything's done proper" idea for ten years, plus a playful social presence. Volume share rose from 18% to 33% while keeping a price premium, and group turnover reached £319.2M (FY24, +8%). It overtook PG Tips without discounting.

For LMTY: pick one line ("The café cup, without the café") and repeat it everywhere for years. Personality beats discounting.

AriZona: price as the brand

Price anchorPackagingDon Vultaggio launched 23-oz art cans at 99¢ in 1992 and has never raised the price. About 1 billion 99-cent cans a year make up roughly 25% of revenue. Thinner cans and logistics kept the margin. Printing the price on the can stopped retailers marking it up.

For LMTY: a memorable price point ("under $1 a cup") is itself marketing. Put the per-cup number on the label, not just the site.

Twinings: heritage plus premium margin

HeritageMargin£234.3M revenue with £99.1M pre-tax profit (FY24) is a margin most brands never reach. Twinings earns it on 300 years of history, a Royal Warrant, a wide flavor range and gifting. ABF reports volume growth in the UK, US and France driven by more marketing spend.

For LMTY: trust lets you charge more. Without heritage, borrow trust: verified reviews, transparent sourcing, a founder's face.

Pure Leaf and Gold Peak: an emotional benefit in a big channel

DistributionSimple benefitPure Leaf ($988M, 21% share) sells "real brewed tea" and is adding zero sugar. Gold Peak sells "tastes like homemade" and posted 24 straight quarters of double-digit growth to cross $1B. Both are one plain promise plus PepsiCo or Coke trucks.

For LMTY: the promise must be plain and true. When you reach retail, partner rather than build the trucks yourself.

Celestial Seasonings and Yogi: owning a need state

OccasionNamingSleepytime means bedtime. Yogi names its teas by the need (Detox, Bedtime, Stomach Ease) and puts a quote on every tag; it grew 10–15% a year. The shopper buys the outcome, not the leaf. Note that Hain is now cutting its 91 blends to fewer than 55, because too many flavors diluted the brand.

For LMTY: own "the cozy cup when you're run down", worded as a feeling, not a medical claim. Add flavors slowly.

Tazo and the medicine ball: a café drink became a grocery product

Café haloTazo grew inside Starbucks (bought for $8.1M in 1999) and sold for $384M in 2017 with $112.5M in sales. The medicine-ball drink went viral as a secret-menu item in 2016 and was added to the menu in 2017; Tazo's concentrate brought it to grocery stores. That is the demand LMTY is riding.

For LMTY: the occasion is proven, but the incumbent owns the "real" version. Differentiate on format (portable, zero sugar), never on "same as Tazo".

Dilmah and Ahmad: a challenger story abroad

StoryMarket entryDilmah started in Australia and New Zealand (in 2013 about 10% of retail sales) with single-origin tea and the founder's own face, then grew to about 100 countries. Ahmad picked tea-loving markets the giants underserved (Middle East, CIS) and became top 3 there.

For LMTY: start where the incumbents are weakest. That means one channel and one audience (TikTok-native young women in cold-weather US states), not "everyone".

Ito En and Mixue: owning the supply chain

Supply chainIto En invented bottled green tea and controls it from leaf to bottle; sales were ¥472.7B (FY to April 2025). Mixue reached about 60,000 stores and ¥33.6B revenue (+35% in 2025) on ultra-low prices made possible by its own supply chain.

For LMTY: long-term, a white-label bottle is copyable. Own something: a custom formula, a flavor, or exclusive supply terms.

The failures: Snapple, Honest Tea, Teavana

CautionarySnapple grew through small distributors and a quirky voice. Quaker paid $1.7B, swapped the distributors, cut flavors and dropped the voice, then sold it for $300M 27 months later. Honest Tea reached 75,000 outlets with Coca-Cola, then was cut in 2022 when it no longer fit Coke's portfolio. Teavana ($620M) depended on malls; when mall traffic fell, all 379 stores closed and Starbucks took a $102M write-down.

For LMTY: don't depend on one channel (Meta ads are the "mall" here), protect the voice that got you customers, and keep control of the brand.

Liquid I.V.: the closest format analog

FormatDTC to retailA drink mix sold on one simple benefit, grown through DTC and Amazon, then Costco and mass retail. Unilever bought it in 2020 at about $200M revenue; it is now near $1B and Unilever's biggest wellness brand. Its category (powdered mixes) grew 20% in 2024.

For LMTY: this is the path. Prove repeat purchase online, win Amazon, then go to retail with the data.

10 things LMTY should do, in order

  1. Lock down the product facts before any more ads

    Get the supplier's full formula, a Supplement/Nutrition Facts panel, the sugar and caffeine numbers and a certificate of analysis. Change the page wherever a claim isn't confirmed. Then the label and the site say the same thing.

    From: Twinings, Pure Leaf (trust is the premium) · Snapple/Teavana (one bad shock ends a brand)

  2. Own one moment and say it the same way for a year

    "The café honey-lemon cup, in 10 seconds, anywhere." Use it in every ad, the hero, the label and the unboxing. No new taglines for 12 months.

    From: Yorkshire Tea (one idea, 10 years, share 18%→33%)

  3. Make "under $1 a cup" the headline price

    Lead with per-cup cost against the $5+ café cup, and print it on the bottle. Keep bundles as the main offer so the average order clears $40.

    From: AriZona (price as the brand)

  4. Get 50 real reviews in 60 days

    Send free bottles to the first buyers and to 20–30 micro-creators with honest-review terms and #ad disclosure. Install a review app (Judge.me) so the rating bar fills itself. Never fabricate a single one (FTC: about $53,000 per fake review).

    From: Dilmah (founder face), Liquid I.V. (creator-driven growth)

  5. Fix the unit economics before scaling spend

    Know your landed cost per bottle, pick/pack/ship cost and refund rate, and set a maximum CPA per bundle. The research report showed one-bottle orders lose money on paid traffic; push the 3- and 6-bottle bundles and a subscription.

    From: Twinings (42% pre-tax margin), Lipton (debt and falling revenue)

  6. Turn on Subscribe & Save once repeat buyers appear

    Add a subscription app (Loop or Recharge) after you see people reordering, at 10% off. A drop-a-day product runs out in 30 days, which suits a monthly plan.

    From: Liquid I.V., Pinemoor (recurring revenue)

  7. Separate the brands

    Archive the tea-concentrate product or move it off the LMTY name. Rename the store to LMTY everywhere (it still says "Lumenity" in Shopify). One brand, one product, one promise.

    From: Hain Celestial (cutting 91 blends to under 55), Snapple (focus)

  8. Plan around the season

    Concentrate ad spend and creator posts from October to March (cold weather, holidays, "feeling run down"), with an iced-summer angle as the off-season. Build inventory before October.

    From: Snapple (winter-sales insight that launched AriZona), Celestial (bedtime occasion)

  9. Win Amazon next, then one retailer

    After DTC proves repeat purchase, list on Amazon with the same bundles. Use the sales data to pitch one regional chain or Costco roadshow. Don't open many channels at once.

    From: Liquid I.V. (DTC → Amazon → Costco → $1B), Teavana (one channel = one point of failure)

  10. Make the formula your own

    Once you sell 1,000+ bottles a month, commission a custom flavor or formula (e.g., real lemon juice concentrate plus ginger) with exclusivity in the supplier contract. That is the thing a copycat cannot buy from the same factory.

    From: Ito En, Mixue, Dilmah (controlling supply)
