1. Executive summary

$0.37per serving on the 6-bottle bundle
$4.45+a grande Starbucks Honey Citrus Mint (2024)
$1.5BUS drink-mix category, +20% in 2024
~$0.15per serving for MiO / Stur water drops

The opportunity. People already pay $4–5 for the café "medicine ball" (Honey Citrus Mint Tea), and the drink-mix category is growing fast because of #WaterTok and the Liquid I.V. effect. A lemon wellness dropper that makes that cozy cup in 10 seconds, anywhere, has a real place: cheaper than the café, more convenient than a tea bag, more "wellness" than a water enhancer.

The catch. LMTY sits between three cheaper or better-known options: Lipton/Tazo (familiar, cheap), MiO/Stur (about a quarter of LMTY's price per serving) and Starbucks (the original). It wins only if the brand makes people feel the cozy, self-care moment, not just "lemon flavor in water". Price per serving must be framed against the café, never against water enhancers.

What decides success: (1) a memorable, consistent ritual brand, (2) creator-led proof and reviews, (3) bundles and subscriptions that make the first order profitable enough, (4) owning a formula or flavor competitors can't buy from the same factory.

2. What the product is, and isn't

It is

  • A flavor-and-ritual concentrate: one dropper (≈2 mL) in hot or cold water makes a lemon "cozy cup".
  • Portable: fits in a bag, desk drawer or carry-on; no kettle, no steeping.
  • Lightly functional in feel: green tea note, zero sugar (to be confirmed), warm-lemon comfort.
  • Replenishable: one bottle lasts about a month at one cup a day, a natural subscription rhythm.

It isn't

  • A medicine or cold remedy. It must never be sold as one (FDA/FTC).
  • A tea bag or brewed tea. Tea purists won't see it as "real tea".
  • A cheap water enhancer. If shoppers file it next to MiO, they'll think it's overpriced.
  • A one-off novelty. The business works only if people reorder.

Open decision: food or dietary supplement? FDA judges by the overall product (label, marketing, composition). Words like "drink" or "water" and comparisons to beverages point to a conventional food (Nutrition Facts panel, claims limited to taste and nutrition). A Supplement Facts panel allows structure/function claims but requires the FDA disclaimer and supplement manufacturing rules. Decide this with the supplier and a regulatory consultant before printing final labels; it changes what the site may say.

5. Target customer

Primary: "The café-cup regular" — women 18–34, US

Today: buys Starbucks or similar 2–4× a week; knows the medicine ball; spends without thinking.

Job to be done: "When I'm run down or want a cozy moment, I want that warm honey-lemon cup without leaving or spending $5."

Where: TikTok, Instagram Reels, Pinterest; Target and Amazon shopper.

Triggers: first cold snap, cold-and-flu season, long workdays, self-care Sundays.

Secondary: "The on-the-go worker" — nurses, teachers, office workers, students, travelers

Job to be done: "I want something warm and better than plain water at work or on the road, with no kitchen."

Where: workplace word of mouth, LinkedIn/IG creators in nursing and teaching, travel content.

Gift buyer (seasonal)

Job to be done: "A small, thoughtful 'feel better / stay cozy' gift." Holiday bundles and care packages.

Not the target: habitual tea-bag drinkers who shop on price, tea purists, and people looking for a cold remedy.

Seasonality

Demand peaks with cold weather (October–March). Plan inventory and most ad spend for that window, with an "iced lemon" summer angle to keep sales steady.

Pricing levers

  • Keep the bundle as the default (6-bottle pre-selected) and push average order value above $40.
  • Add Subscribe & Save at 10% once people reorder; subscriptions churn 4–7% a month in replenishment categories.
  • Never compete on price with MiO/Stur; compare only to the café cup.
  • Test a $14.99 "trial" single on Amazon only, to lower the first-purchase barrier there.

10. Brand and messaging

Say

  • "The café cozy cup, in 10 seconds."
  • "Under $1 a cup. Skip the drive-thru."
  • "Hot or iced. Desk, gym, car, carry-on."
  • "Your feel-good ritual for long days and cold nights."
  • "Zero sugar" (only once the formula confirms it).

Never say

  • "Cures colds", "fights flu", "boosts immunity", "cancel out flu season".
  • "Medicine ball" in ads (Starbucks' customer nickname; trademark and claim risk).
  • "Same as Tazo/Starbucks" or use their names, logos or footage.
  • Fake or "confirmed" ratings and reviews (FTC: ~$53K per violation).
